# Safety and Piston Singles — Voice Script

Updated 06-Nov-2026. Storyboard: Claude Doc "Safety and Piston Singles — Video Storyboard".

One file per line, named exactly as shown, `.m4a` or `.mp3`. Leave about half a second of quiet at each end. Numbers are written the way to say them.

## Part A — Narration

### `s0-open.m4a` — 0 · Cold open

At the turn of the century, piston single-engine airplanes flying under Part 91 in the US had about 147 fatal accidents a year. Over the last three years: about 56. The registered fleet shrank too, so is that real progress, or just fewer airplanes? Let's look at the numbers. Just the facts. The conclusions are yours.

### `s2-sources.m4a` — 2 · What's counted · sources

Everything here comes from two public sources: the NTSB accident database and the FAA aircraft registry. GA Accident Stats, one of my GeezerGeek Pilot apps, refreshes both every month.

### `s2-yardstick.m4a` — 2 · What's counted · yardstick

That's 38 piston-single model families from six makers, Part 91 flying only. The yardstick is accidents per thousand registered airplanes per year. Not per flight hour. So an airplane that flies a lot will look worse than one that mostly sits in the hangar. Keep that in mind.

### `s3-trend.m4a` — 3 · Trend · the line

Here's the fatal accident rate for all piston singles, as a three-year average. In 2000 it was about one fatal accident per thousand airplanes per year. By 2009 it was about point six three, roughly a third lower. Then it sat on a plateau for well over a decade. In the last three years it dropped again, to about point four four.

### `s3-footnotes.m4a` — 3 · Trend · footnotes

Two footnotes. Registry counts before about 2011 still include plenty of airplanes that weren't flying, so the early rates actually read low: the real improvement is likely bigger than this line shows. And the latest drop is new: 2026 is counted through October and prorated to a full year. It isn't only this app's numbers, though. The FAA reports fiscal 2024 had the lowest general aviation fatal accident rate since it began tracking in 2009. Worth watching.

### `s3-all.m4a` — 3 · Trend · all accidents

All accidents, not just fatal ones, fell too: from about 850 a year to under 500.

### `s4-intro.m4a` — 4 · Funnel · intro

So the whole category improved. But how does one type compare with the rest? That's what a funnel plot is for.

### `s4-history.m4a` — 4 · Funnel · where it comes from

It comes from medicine. Statistician David Spiegelhalter developed it in the early 2000s to compare hospitals' surgical results, and it was used in England's reviews of children's heart surgery, so a small unit wasn't condemned for a run of bad luck.

### `s4-axes.m4a` — 4 · Funnel · axes and average

Each dot will be one airplane type. Left to right is fleet size, from a few dozen airplanes to over twenty thousand Skyhawks. Up and down is the fatal accident rate, 2000 to today. The dashed line is the piston-single average: point six nine.

### `s4-coin.m4a` — 4 · Funnel · why a funnel

Why a funnel? Flip a coin ten times and seven heads means nothing. Flip it a thousand times and seven hundred heads tells you something's up. A small fleet is the ten flips: one or two accidents swing its rate a lot. A big fleet is the thousand flips.

### `s4-limits.m4a` — 4 · Funnel · limits

The shaded band is where a type with exactly average safety would land 95 percent of the time, given its fleet size. The dashed curves are 99.8 percent. A dot outside the band differs from the average by more than chance. A dot inside, even one far from the line, may just be luck.

### `s4-dots.m4a` — 4 · Funnel · dots in

Below the funnel: trainers, including the venerable Skyhawk, and older taildraggers. Above it, mostly high-performance travelers.

### `s4-caveat.m4a` — 4 · Funnel · the caveat

And this is the important part. Outside the funnel means different, not safer or less safe. These are rates per registered airplane, not per hour and not per mission. The funnel shows you where to look. Why a type sits where it does is for you to work out.

### `s5-category.m4a` — 5 · Still to do · category

Even at the better rate, this category still has fifty-some fatal accidents a year. Where are they? Loss of control in flight, including stall-spin, is a third of all fatal accidents since 2000, by far the largest group. Then controlled flight into terrain at about eleven percent, powerplant failures at ten, weather at eight.

### `s5-phase.m4a` — 5 · Still to do · phase

By phase of flight: a third en route, a fifth while maneuvering, including low-altitude flying, and about fifteen percent each on initial climb and on approach. Each accident gets one defining event. It groups similar accidents together; it isn't the cause.

### `s6-tour.m4a` — 6 · Try it yourself

Every number here comes from the app, and you can run it for your own airplane. Here's mine, the Diamond DA40: inside the funnel, right near the average. Any type against any other, by years, purpose, category and phase of flight, down to each NTSB report.
